A Voice in the Static

You crouch behind a metal shelving unit and turn the radio dial, carefully adjusting the frequency to avoid making too much noise. At first, the device produces nothing but a harsh burst of static. You turn the dial again, passing through fragments of distorted voices and what sounds like an emergency announcement recorded years ago. A sudden spike of interference makes you flinch, and you nearly drop the radio onto the concrete floor. Then a voice breaks through. "Can anyone hear me? Please respond." The speaker sounds exhausted, their words interrupted by bursts of static. You press the transmit button and whisper that you are trapped inside Blackwood Hospital and need a way out. For several seconds, there is no response. Then the voice returns, quieter than before. "Listen carefully. Do not go into the basement. Whatever you hear down there, do not open that door." You glance at the warning written on the wall. Before you can ask what is in the basement, the voice continues. The speaker explains that an old service tunnel connects the boiler room to the hospital's maintenance corridors. It was once used to transport supplies and dispose of medical waste, but the tunnel was sealed after a series of unexplained incidents. According to the voice, the locks controlling several of the lower-floor doors are connected to an electrical control box near the tunnel entrance. "If the doctor reaches the basement before you cut the power, you're finished," the speaker warns. "He knows you're awake. He's been waiting for this." A sudden metallic scrape interrupts the transmission. You freeze as the radio picks up the sound of someone dragging keys across a door. The speaker begins breathing heavily. "He's here," they whisper. "I thought the locks would hold. I thought—" The transmission cuts out. You try to call back, but the radio produces only static. Then a new voice emerges from the speaker. It is deeper, slower, and unmistakably familiar. "You should not listen to strangers." The doctor's voice is coming through the radio. You drop the device onto the shelf and stare at it in disbelief. The radio's frequency indicator begins moving on its own, cycling rapidly through different channels until it settles on a faint, continuous tone. Somewhere beyond the supply room, a door slams shut. You now know that the service tunnel may provide an escape route, but the transmission has raised a disturbing question: who was speaking to you, and how did the doctor gain access to the same frequency? Before you can decide, a key scrapes against the supply room door.
